The framework

Four questions, one intersection

Profitable software rarely comes from one circle. A thing you love that nobody needs is a hobby. A thing that pays but you hate is a grind. Ikigai is the small region where all four agree — and that's exactly where durable, profitable products live.

What you love

The problems you'd still poke at on a Sunday. Sustains you through the 18 unglamorous months before traction.

What you're good at

Your unfair advantage — a craft, a domain, an audience. It's your moat and your speed.

What the world needs

A real, felt, recurring pain. If people already hack around it in spreadsheets, that's demand.

What pays you

Someone with a budget and urgency. Businesses beat consumers here; urgency beats "nice to have."

The profitable part

An idea isn't a business until money moves

Ikigai points the direction; this is how you make it pay. The order matters — most first-time builders spend months coding before they've confirmed anyone will pay. Invert it.

Week 1
1

Find 20 people with the pain

Pick one narrow customer from your matches. Talk to 20 of them. You're listening for the same frustration said three different ways.

Week 2
2

Pre-sell before you build

Offer to solve it manually or with a landing page + price. A card entered, a deposit, a signed LOI — that's validation. A "cool idea!" is not.

Weeks 3–6
3

Ship the thinnest slice

Build only the one workflow they'll pay for. Charge from day one — free users teach you nothing about willingness to pay.

Ongoing
4

Price for value, then raise it

Recurring > one-off. B2B tolerates 5–20× consumer prices. When nobody blinks at your price, it's too low.

What makes software actually profitable in 2026

  • Recurring revenue. A $50/mo B2B subscription with low churn beats a $500 one-time sale within a year — and compounds.
  • Narrow beats broad. "Scheduling for tattoo studios" out-earns "scheduling for everyone." A specific customer will pay a premium to feel understood.
  • Sell to budgets, not wallets. Businesses buy tools that save staff hours or make money; consumers resist paying for almost anything.
  • AI as margin, not the product. Use AI to remove a labor cost inside a real workflow — don't sell "AI." Customers pay for the outcome, not the model.
  • Distribution is half the product. Pick a customer you can already reach — your job, your community, an audience you can build. Great software nobody finds earns $0.
